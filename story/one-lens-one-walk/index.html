One lens, one walk

Use a familiar angle of view as a working constraint, without turning a simple exercise into a rule about equipment.

Sina Afshar
Reading view

Give the walk a boundary

Taking one lens, or leaving a zoom at one position, removes a decision that can otherwise interrupt observation. The point is not that changing lenses is wrong. It is that a temporary constraint can make the relationship between distance, framing and subject easier to notice. Choose a setting that feels practical for the route.

Set a modest aim before leaving: a dozen careful frames, one neighbourhood or half an hour. A boundary makes it easier to recognise what the exercise taught. It also discourages the idea that the walk must produce an important photograph. Repeated, ordinary observation is useful even on a day when no finished image emerges.

Move to change relationships

With a fixed angle of view, moving your feet changes how near and distant objects relate in the frame. Watch the background while approaching a subject, not only the subject's size. A doorway may begin to overlap a person, or a quiet wall may replace a complicated street behind them. Those changes can determine whether the photograph is clear.

Some viewpoints will remain inaccessible or inappropriate. Do not enter a road, private space or difficult terrain just to satisfy the constraint. If the picture needs another lens or a safer position, recognise that fact and move on. The exercise is meant to improve judgement, not to override it.

Vary more than distance

A fixed lens does not require a sequence of pictures made from the same height. Try looking along a surface, placing a foreground element near the edge or allowing more space above a subject. Notice how these choices change the viewer's sense of position. Repetition becomes useful when it reveals different relationships rather than producing duplicates.

Work with both simple and complicated scenes. In a sparse frame, the challenge may be deciding where a single object belongs. In a dense one, it may be finding a clear path through several layers. Keeping the lens constant makes those compositional decisions more visible when the photographs are reviewed together.

Review the contact sheet

Arrange the results at a similar viewing size. Mark the frames in which you moved with a clear purpose, then mark those in which the composition appears to have been accepted too quickly. Look for repeated habits: subjects always in the middle, backgrounds ignored or more empty space than the scene needs. The pattern matters more than the number of successful frames.

Repeat the walk another day with a different angle of view. Compare the kinds of pictures that each encourages without declaring a winner. Equipment becomes easier to choose when it is connected to actual ways of seeing. A useful lens is one whose possibilities and limitations you understand well enough to work with deliberately.
