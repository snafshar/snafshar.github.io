Water, motion and the choice of a moment

Choose how movement should read before reaching for a shutter-speed recipe.

Sina Afshar
Reading view

Watch the water before photographing it

A shoreline is not a single repeating event. Water gathers, breaks, spreads and withdraws, with different shapes visible at each stage. Spend a few minutes watching the pattern from a safe position. Decide whether the photograph concerns the impact of a wave, the lines left behind it or the quieter interval between movements.

Keep safety separate from the desire for a viewpoint. Conditions can change, wet surfaces can be slippery and a place that seems calm can still be exposed. Choose a position with a safe route away and do not move closer simply because a photograph appears to need a more dramatic foreground. A useful frame never requires ignoring the actual setting.

Decide what motion should look like

A shorter exposure can preserve more of the visible structure in moving water. A longer one can combine changing shapes into streaks or a smoother surface. Neither treatment is automatically more natural or expressive. The appropriate choice depends on what the movement contributes to the picture.

There is no single shutter speed that produces the same appearance for every wave, waterfall or stream. The speed and direction of movement, the camera's viewpoint and the final viewing size all matter. Make a short series and examine the results. Use the comparison to choose a description of motion rather than relying on a number detached from the scene.

Keep a stable reference

A still element can make motion easier to read. A rock, pier or distant headland can provide a point of comparison with the changing water. Check whether that reference remains clear in the frame. If both the water and the surrounding structure look indistinct, the intended effect may be difficult to understand.

When using a longer exposure, support the camera appropriately and inspect the stationary parts of the photograph. Also watch the bright areas where foam or reflections gather. A pleasing flow of lines may still compete with the main subject if it becomes the brightest feature in the image. Composition and timing continue to matter after the exposure method is chosen.

Choose a phase as well as a duration

Make three sets from the same safe viewpoint: one as water approaches, one near the peak of the movement and one as it recedes. Keep the composition broadly consistent so that the changing shape is easy to compare. The exercise may reveal that the retreating pattern is more interesting than the most dramatic splash.

Select the frame in which the motion supports the still elements and the still elements help explain the motion. Write a sentence describing what that particular moment contributes. Water photographs become more individual when they are built around an observed relationship, rather than around an effect that could be applied to almost any stretch of coast.
