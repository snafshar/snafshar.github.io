Black and white is about tones

Look beyond the absence of colour and build a photograph around separation, texture and visual weight.

Sina Afshar
Reading view

Colour and brightness tell different stories

Two objects can have very different colours and still reproduce at similar brightness in black and white. A colourful scene therefore does not automatically become a clear monochrome photograph. Before converting it, look for the relationships that will remain: a pale face against a dark coat, a soft sky above hard stone or a bright line crossing a quiet surface.

Ask why the photograph should lose its colour. Perhaps colour is carrying information that the picture needs. Perhaps it competes with a gesture that becomes clearer without it. Conversion is most useful when it gives the photograph a more exact language, not when it serves as a general treatment for an uncertain frame.

Arrange visual weight

Large dark areas can make a frame feel grounded or enclosed. Small bright areas can attract attention quickly. Neither is automatically a subject. Examine whether the distribution of those areas supports the place where you want the eye to pause. A bright corner that has little descriptive value may need a different composition.

Keep intermediate tones in mind as well. A photograph made only of hard extremes can feel forceful, but it may also flatten subtle surfaces. Mist, skin, weathered plaster and water often depend on small transitions. Let the scene tell you how much separation it needs instead of making every subject equally dramatic.

Edit locally and with a purpose

Begin with a neutral conversion, then decide which part of the image needs help. If an architectural edge is difficult to distinguish, a modest local adjustment may be enough. If the sky dominates the subject, reduce its insistence rather than making everything else louder. Change one relationship at a time so that you can explain the effect.

Watch for halos around boundaries and for textures that become brittle. Strong contrast can look persuasive on a small screen while making a larger image uncomfortable to read. Revisit the file at more than one viewing size. Also return to the unedited version occasionally; familiarity with an adjustment can make an excessive treatment seem normal.

Make a tonal study

Photograph three objects with different colours against the same background. Convert the frames to black and white and note which objects remain distinct. Then repeat the exercise with a change in lighting direction or background brightness. The comparison will teach more about separation than applying a collection of presets.

For a finished selection, choose a frame whose subject remains understandable at thumbnail size and whose surfaces still reward a closer look. Write down one element that colour was doing and one element that monochrome now does. That small explanation helps keep the edit connected to the photograph rather than to a fashionable appearance.
