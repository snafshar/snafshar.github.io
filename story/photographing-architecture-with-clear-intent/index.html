Photographing architecture with clear intent

Work with viewpoint, vertical lines and human scale before deciding how to correct an architectural image.

Sina Afshar
Reading view

Decide what the building is doing

A building can be a volume in space, a pattern of openings, a surface marked by weather or a setting for daily movement. Trying to describe all of those qualities in one frame can produce an image with no clear priority. Begin with a sentence about the relationship you want to show.

If the subject is the rhythm of windows, a quieter viewpoint may be useful. If the subject is the height of a tower, an upward view may be the most direct description. A photograph intended to explain a room may need a different treatment from one concerned with how that room feels. The purpose should guide the geometry.

Use viewpoint before correction

Notice whether the camera is tilted and how that affects the apparent direction of vertical lines. When a more parallel rendering is wanted, a more level camera position can simplify the starting point. Changing distance or height may also change which parts of the building overlap. These choices affect the photograph before any editing begins.

Perspective adjustments can be useful, but they also require decisions about cropping and proportions. Leave room around important edges when you expect to make a correction. Afterwards, look at the entire structure rather than only checking whether one line is straight. A technically tidy image can still feel awkward if the building's relationships have become unconvincing.

Keep evidence of scale

A doorway, a chair or a person can help a viewer understand size. Without such a reference, a close view of a façade may become an abstract pattern. That abstraction can be effective, but it should be chosen. Consider whether the image needs to describe the experience of moving through the place.

Human presence can also introduce a different timescale. A worn stair and a passing foot connect a permanent structure to a brief action. Wait for a position that clarifies rather than obscures the space. An empty building is not inherently more architectural; sometimes an ordinary use reveals the design more clearly than an immaculate view.

Make three descriptions

Choose an accessible building and make one image of its overall form, one of a repeated detail and one of its relationship to people or the street. Keep each frame focused on its own task. This exercise replaces the search for a single definitive angle with a small, coherent set of observations.

Review the three images together. Does the detail explain something that the wider frame cannot? Does the contextual image show how the place is used? Remove any frame that merely repeats another at a slightly different distance. A concise architectural sequence can provide a fuller description than a large collection of nearly identical views.
