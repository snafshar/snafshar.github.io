Reflections without perfect symmetry

Use water, windows and polished surfaces to create relationships rather than merely duplicate a subject.

Sina Afshar
Reading view

Look for what the reflection changes

A reflection is interesting because it changes a view as much as because it repeats one. Ripples break an outline into fragments. A window combines the street behind you with a room beyond the glass. A puddle compresses a large building into a small, uneven surface. Start by identifying that transformation.

Perfect symmetry is one possible composition, but it is not the only one. A narrow strip of reflection can suggest a larger scene outside the frame. An incomplete reflection may make the viewer compare what is present with what is missing. Let the surface contribute a distinct voice rather than assuming that more duplication means a stronger photograph.

Choose a height deliberately

Small changes in camera height can change the amount of reflected information. Near a puddle, lowering the camera may reveal a roof that was invisible from standing height. Raising it may show the texture of the surface instead. Move carefully and check the edges, particularly where reflected forms meet the surrounding pavement or shoreline.

Decide whether the reflecting surface should be obvious. Including a few stones, a window frame or a patch of floating leaves can explain the geometry. Excluding those clues can make the image more ambiguous. Both choices can work, but accidental fragments at the edge often leave the viewer unsure in a less useful way.

Let movement describe the surface

Watch several cycles of movement before choosing a moment. Water may briefly become smooth between gusts, or a passing vehicle may change the light on a shop window. A reflection that seems stable at first glance can contain many different compositions over a minute. Waiting helps distinguish a recognisable pattern from a lucky distraction.

When photographing glass, consider your own position and reflection. A dark area behind the camera may reduce some unwanted reflections, while a different angle can reveal others. Do not assume that every reflection should be eliminated. Sometimes the overlap between inside and outside is the whole subject of the photograph.

Try an unequal pair

Make two photographs of the same reflection. In the first, give the original subject most of the frame. In the second, let the reflection dominate and include only a small clue to the object above or beside it. Compare how the balance changes the mood and how much explanation each version requires.

Select the frame in which the real surface and its reflected world depend on each other. That may be the most symmetrical image, but it may equally be the one interrupted by a ripple or a rough edge. A useful reflection photograph offers more than a second copy: it gives the first subject a different setting in which to be understood.
