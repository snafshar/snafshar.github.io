Overcast days and quiet colour

A grey sky can be an invitation to work with subtle surfaces, restrained contrasts and smaller scenes.

Sina Afshar
Reading view

Change the question, not the weather

An overcast day can feel disappointing when the photograph in mind depends on a bright horizon or a sharp shadow. Rather than waiting for the same image under different conditions, ask which subjects suit the light that is actually available. Weathered paint, foliage, stone and small gestures may become easier to describe without a competing patch of sunlight.

A grey sky does not make every part of a place identical. Look under awnings, beside windows and at the edges of open spaces. Nearby walls and trees can still create local differences in brightness and colour. The most useful observation may be a small change within the scene rather than a dramatic change in the sky.

Find separation in the background

Without strong shadows, the background becomes especially important. A pale object against a pale wall may need a different angle before it feels distinct. Move until its outline crosses a darker area, or choose a simpler arrangement of surrounding shapes. The camera cannot invent a clear relationship that the viewpoint has not supplied.

Resist compensating for every quiet scene with aggressive editing. Gentle transitions can be the quality that makes the photograph worth keeping. Instead of adding contrast everywhere, identify the one boundary that helps the subject read. A small, deliberate adjustment often preserves the atmosphere more successfully than turning every surface into a graphic statement.

Watch colour relationships

Soft light can make modest differences in colour more noticeable. A faded blue door, an ochre wall and a green plant may form a picture without a single brilliant highlight. Look for a dominant colour and a smaller supporting one. Too many equally insistent patches can make an otherwise quiet frame feel unsettled.

Check white balance with the intended mood in mind, while keeping familiar materials believable. A cool interpretation may suit a damp morning; a warmer one may suit an interior. Neither should be an automatic correction applied to every photograph from the day. Compare neighbouring images if they are going to form a sequence.

Work at a smaller scale

For a short exercise, avoid including the sky in your next ten frames. Look for subjects between arm's length and the opposite side of a narrow street. This constraint directs attention towards textures, edges and arrangements that might disappear in a wider view. It also tests whether the photograph really needs an expansive setting.

At the end of the walk, choose a frame that would lose something under hard sunlight. Explain what the diffused light allowed you to see. The aim is not to claim that one kind of weather is best. It is to recognise that a useful photographic day begins with a responsive way of looking, not with a particular forecast.
