Reading the light before lifting the camera

A practical way to recognise direction, softness and contrast before choosing a composition.

Sina Afshar
Reading view

Begin with the shadows

The most useful first question is often not what to photograph, but what the light is doing. Look at the shadow beneath a bench, the bright edge of a roof or the transition across a face. Those small areas reveal direction and contrast more clearly than a dramatic sky. Before changing a setting, decide whether the subject needs to feel quiet, sharp, spacious or enclosed.

Walk around the subject when access permits. A wall that looks flat from one position may reveal its texture a few steps to the side. A translucent leaf can change character completely when the light passes through it. This is not an instruction to find the universally best angle. It is a way to understand which qualities each angle makes visible.

Separate direction from softness

Side light can describe shape through bright and dark surfaces. Frontal light can simplify those differences and make colour or gesture more prominent. Backlighting can separate an outline from its surroundings, although it may also make the subject darker than expected. Each arrangement offers a different description of the same scene.

Softness is a separate decision. Broad, diffused light tends to make transitions gradual; a small, direct source tends to draw harder boundaries. The weather alone does not tell the whole story. A doorway, a shaded arcade or a nearby bright wall can change the local light. Inspect the place where the subject actually stands.

Choose what must retain detail

A bright window and a shaded interior may exceed what one exposure can describe comfortably. Decide which information carries the picture. If the view outside is essential, protect it and accept a darker room. If a person inside is the subject, their visibility may matter more than a featureless patch of sky. Check the result instead of treating a meter reading as a final judgement.

Exposure warnings and the histogram can help identify areas approaching the limits of the recorded image. They do not decide how the photograph should feel. A silhouette can be intentional, and a small reflection can be brilliant. What matters is whether losing detail supports the subject or distracts from it.

Try a ten-minute observation

Choose one ordinary subject and make three frames from different directions. Keep the subject recognisable in all three. Afterwards, describe what changed without using the words better or worse: softer edge, stronger texture, clearer outline, quieter background. This vocabulary turns a vague preference into a decision you can repeat.

Finish by choosing one frame and writing a sentence about why its light suits the subject. The aim is not to collect a formula for every situation. It is to slow the moment between noticing something and pressing the shutter. Once that habit becomes familiar, the camera settings have a much clearer job to do.
